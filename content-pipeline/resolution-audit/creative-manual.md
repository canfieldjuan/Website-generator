# Resolution Audit Creative Manual

Use this document to help models create content for The Resolution Audit:
social posts, Reddit analysis, blog posts, landing-page copy, sales notes, ad
ideas, and image prompts.

This manual is creative source material, not the final claim authority. When it
conflicts with `source-pack.md` or `claims-guard.md`, those files win.

## Compact Context Block

Use this block when a smaller model cannot handle the full kit.

```text
The Resolution Audit analyzes a support-ticket CSV and turns repeated customer
questions into a source-backed action queue.

It does not promise savings, ticket reduction, automatic help-center updates,
or automatic support replies. It estimates cost exposure and shows what the
ticket history appears to support.

The strongest frame is not "AI writes FAQs." The strongest frame is "your
support queue contains repeat problems, and some of them belong to product,
billing, policy, or process owners who may never see the support tool."

The report separates:
- repeated questions with evidence-backed draft answers
- repeated questions with no proven answer in the ticket history
- issues that look like documentation gaps
- issues that may need product, policy, process, or billing review

Write in a plain, diagnostic voice. Be specific. Use mechanisms. Avoid hype.
Use "may," "appears," "estimated," and "probable" where evidence is not
certain.
```

## Product Narrative

The product is an audit of repeated support work.

Most support reporting tells a team what happened: ticket count, handle time,
CSAT, bot containment, or top tags. The Resolution Audit asks a more useful
question:

What keeps repeating, what evidence do we already have, what is it costing to
answer again and again, and who needs to act next?

The important turn:

- A repeated question is not automatically a content problem.
- A resolved ticket is not automatically a resolved root cause.
- A bot deflection is not automatically proof that the customer got unstuck.
- A help article is not always the right fix.

The audit earns trust when it is willing to say, "There is no proven answer in
the ticket history." That sentence is not a failure. It is the boundary between
useful diagnosis and invented content.

## Core Creative Tension

Use these tensions as the engine of posts and copy.

### Tension 1: Support Owns The Queue, Not Always The Cause

Support sees the repeat issue first because customers send it there. But the
fix may live in product, billing, policy, onboarding, documentation, or
operations.

Safe wording:

- "The expensive repeat is not always a support problem."
- "Support may be the smoke alarm, not the fire."
- "The team answering the ticket may not be the team that can remove the
  ticket."

Avoid:

- Saying a specific team definitely owns the issue without evidence.
- Turning the post into blame.

### Tension 2: Resolved Is Not The Same As Fixed

Agents can resolve the same question hundreds of times. That is good service,
but it can hide a system that keeps generating the same work.

Safe wording:

- "A resolved ticket and a fixed problem are not the same thing."
- "The question is not only whether support handled it. The question is why it
  came back."
- "If the same question keeps returning, the answer may be missing, buried, or
  blocked by a workflow issue."

### Tension 3: Content Is Sometimes The Wrong Cure

Some repeats can become review-ready help content. Others need an internal
handoff.

Safe wording:

- "Some findings become drafted answers. Some become operational gaps."
- "The report should not write a help article for a broken workflow."
- "The useful output is not more content. It is knowing when content can carry
  the fix."

### Tension 4: Cost Exposure Beats Topic Lists

A list of repeated topics is easy to ignore. A ranked queue with estimated cost
exposure, source evidence, and probable owner lane is easier to route.

Safe wording:

- "A topic list says what repeated. An audit says what may be worth fixing."
- "Cost exposure turns the repeated question into an operating conversation."
- "The goal is not a bigger dashboard. It is a better next action."

## Voice Rules

Write like an operator who has been inside the queue.

Use:

- short paragraphs
- concrete nouns
- "I am seeing" instead of "everyone knows"
- "in the exports I have reviewed" instead of universal claims
- mechanisms before opinions
- questions that invite operators to disagree

Avoid:

- hype phrases like "revolutionary," "game-changing," or "set it and forget it"
- shame language toward support teams
- certainty about ownership without evidence
- savings promises
- claims that the audit takes over agent work or support tooling

## Content Primitives

These are reusable building blocks. Mix them instead of asking the model to
invent from a blank page.

### Hooks

- "The support metric I trust less every week: resolved tickets."
- "The most expensive repeated question is not always the one support can fix."
- "A help article is a bad answer to a broken workflow."
- "If the bot resolved it, why did the same question come back next week?"
- "The useful support report is not a topic list. It is an ownership question."
- "Some support tickets are product feedback with a timer attached."
- "The dangerous thing about a good support team is that they can hide a broken
  process for a long time."
- "I am trying to stop treating repeated questions like a content calendar."

### Titles

- "Resolved Is Not Fixed"
- "The Support Queue Is A Routing Layer"
- "Stop Turning Every Repeat Into An FAQ"
- "The Question Behind The Question"
- "What Your Bot Deflected But Did Not Resolve"
- "A Better Way To Read Repeated Tickets"
- "The Report Product Actually Forwards"
- "When The Help Article Is The Wrong Fix"

### Contrast Lines

- "Old frame: what should we publish? New frame: what keeps repeating, what
  evidence exists, and who needs to review it?"
- "A tag count gives you volume. An audit gives you evidence and next action."
- "The dashboard says what happened. The audit asks what should change."
- "An answer draft is useful. Knowing not to draft one is sometimes more
  useful."

### Safe Pattern Language

- "A pattern I am testing..."
- "In the exports I have reviewed..."
- "The recurring shape I keep noticing..."
- "I would not call this universal, but it keeps showing up..."
- "This is directional, not a benchmark..."
- "The cost number is exposure, not promised savings..."

### Low-Pressure CTAs

- "Does this match what you see in your queue?"
- "Where would you route this: support, product, billing, policy, or ops?"
- "Would this make you act, or would it feel too accusatory?"
- "If you run support, what would you want the report to show before trusting
  it?"
- "What would make this useful enough to forward?"

## Channel Guidance

### LinkedIn

Best use: point-of-view posts, build-in-public updates, founder/operator
lessons, report-shape feedback.

Shape:

1. Concrete observation.
2. Why the old frame is insufficient.
3. The new mechanism.
4. One grounded example or contrast.
5. Honest limitation.
6. Low-pressure question.

Good LinkedIn stance:

- "I am building this and testing the frame."
- "Here is the trade-off I am wrestling with."
- "Here is the pattern I keep noticing, with qualifiers."

Avoid:

- corporate thought-leadership fog
- fake case studies
- hard selling in the first paragraph

### Reddit

Best use: analysis, genuine questions, build-in-public posts where allowed.

Shape:

1. Lead with the operational question.
2. Share the pattern without making it sound universal.
3. Explain the diagnostic frame.
4. Ask the community how they handle it.

Reddit title patterns:

- "Do you track which repeated tickets support can answer vs. which ones
  product has to fix?"
- "Resolved tickets are starting to feel like a weak support metric. How are
  you measuring repeat problems?"
- "When a bot 'resolves' a question that keeps coming back, what do you count
  that as?"
- "Are repeated support questions a content problem, a product problem, or both?"

Avoid:

- sounding like a landing page
- asking for private exports in the main post unless the community clearly
  allows it
- pretending you have a benchmark if you only have a working sample

### Blog Posts

Best use: category education and durable product framing.

Strong blog structures:

- "Resolved Is Not Fixed"
  - why support metrics miss repeat work
  - how repeated questions become cost exposure
  - when an answer draft is useful
  - when "no proven answer" matters
  - how to route product, policy, process, or documentation gaps

- "Stop Turning Every Repeat Into An FAQ"
  - the old content-calendar reflex
  - why some repeats cannot be solved by content
  - the role of ticket evidence
  - what a diagnostic report should show

- "The Support Queue Is A Routing Layer"
  - support as early warning system
  - the owner-lane problem
  - how evidence changes the conversation
  - what to do before asking for a tool rollout

### Marketing Copy

Best use: landing-page sections, email blocks, one-page explainers, product
descriptions.

Use direct value language:

- "Turn repeated tickets into a source-backed action queue."
- "See which repeats have evidence-backed answers and which ones need a
  different owner."
- "Separate documentation drafts from product, policy, and process gaps."
- "Estimate the cost exposure of repeat support work before deciding what to
  fix."

Use proof language:

- "ranked by repeat volume"
- "anchored to source ticket evidence"
- "review-ready when the ticket history supports it"
- "marked as no proven answer when the evidence is missing"

Avoid:

- "eliminate tickets"
- "guaranteed savings"
- "automate your support center"
- "take over agent work"

### Sales Notes And DMs

Best use: replies after someone expresses interest.

Shape:

1. Acknowledge their context.
2. Explain the Snapshot in one sentence.
3. Make the evidence boundary clear.
4. Offer the next step without pressure.

Template:

```text
That is exactly the kind of pattern the Snapshot is meant to test.

The useful part is not guessing from the outside. It is reading the closed
ticket export to see which questions repeat, whether agents already have a
proven answer, and whether the gap looks like documentation or something a
different owner needs to review.

If the signal is thin, I will say so. If it is strong, you should get a clearer
read on whether the full audit is worth reviewing.
```

## Image And Picture Direction

Use images to make the diagnostic frame tangible. Avoid generic AI robots,
glowing brains, and abstract dashboards.

### Visual Metaphors

- a support queue becoming a routing board
- repeated tickets grouped into lanes: documentation, product, billing, policy,
  process
- a stack of closed tickets with highlighted repeated questions
- a whiteboard showing "resolved" crossed out and "fixed?" circled
- a leak map where each leak has evidence, cost exposure, and owner lane
- a report page with source evidence, not a futuristic cockpit

### Image Prompt Patterns

Use these as starting points for generated pictures:

```text
A realistic editorial illustration of a support operations whiteboard. Closed
support tickets are grouped into lanes labeled documentation, product, billing,
policy, and process. The mood is practical and investigative, not futuristic.
Clean office lighting, readable sticky notes, no brand logos, no people, no
customer names.
```

```text
A sharp product-marketing image of an audit report on a desk. The report shows
ranked repeated support questions, evidence markers, estimated cost exposure,
and probable owner lanes. Modern SaaS operations feel, restrained color palette,
no fake company names, no readable private data.
```

```text
An editorial image for a blog post titled "Resolved Is Not Fixed." A stack of
support tickets has green resolved stamps, but arrows loop back into the queue.
The visual should feel analytical and human, not sci-fi. No customer data or
logos.
```

### Image Safety

Do not show:

- real customer names
- emails, phone numbers, ticket IDs, or screenshots
- fake dashboards with precise numbers that look like customer proof
- bots replacing humans
- visuals that imply automatic help-center publishing

3. `creative-manual.md`.
4. one prompt contract from `prompt-contracts.md`.

Ask for two passes:

1. draft the asset
2. audit the draft against claim risk

### Finetune Lab Mode

Use this manual as training/evaluation source, not as a pile of finished posts.
The useful fine-tune behavior is:

- preserving uncertainty language
- generating sharper hooks without adding unsupported claims
- using the ownership/routing frame
- distinguishing documentation gaps from operational gaps
- creating image prompts that avoid fake customer proof

Useful eval tasks:

- rewrite a flat post into a stronger one without adding claims
- generate five hooks for one angle and rank them by risk
- convert a LinkedIn post into a Reddit analysis post
- produce a blog outline from the same thesis
- flag unsafe lines in a draft and repair them

## Draft Quality Checklist

Before using a draft, check:

1. Does it make one sharp point?
2. Does it use a mechanism, not just a vibe?
3. Does it avoid promised savings or ticket reduction?
4. Does it qualify cost as estimated exposure?
5. Does it qualify ownership as probable or investigatory?
6. Does it treat "no proven answer" as useful evidence?
7. Does it avoid raw customer data?
8. Does it invite a useful reply or next action?

If the draft is safe but boring, add a sharper contrast line. If the draft is
punchy but unsafe, soften the claim before publishing.
